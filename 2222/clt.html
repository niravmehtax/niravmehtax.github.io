1. One population, many samples

Suppose we care about a population of $N$ people and one number for each of them, $X$. Below, $X$ is (variable description). The population has a mean $\mu$ and a variance $\sigma^2$. In real life we never see the whole population. We see a random sample of $n$ people, $X_1, \dots, X_n$, each drawn with equal probability and independently of the others, and we compute the sample mean $$\overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i .$$ In this (contrived) example, we do have the whole population, so we can draw sample after sample and watch what $\overline{X}$ does. In practice this is infeasible: we will typically have one random sample, and so one value, $\overline{x}$. But this simulation lets us compare the distribution of $\overline{X}$ over many samples (which we could never see in practice) with the theoretical distribution implied by the CLT.

3. The Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem describes the distribution of the sample mean for a random sample from any population (with a finite variance), as long as the draws are i.i.d. and $n$ is large. As you can see in the simulation, the shape becomes approximately normal even though the population is not. At $n = 1$ the histogram of sample means is the population. At $n = 5$ or $10$ it is still lopsided on the right. By $n = 30$ or so the normal curve fits. The Central Limit Theorem says that for large $n$, $$Z = \frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\; \normal{0}{1}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \overline{X} \;\overset{\text{approx}}{\sim}\; \normal{\mu}{\sigma^2/n}.$$ Switch to the standardized $z$ view to watch the histogram approach the $\normal{0}{1}$ curve as $n$ grows.

Two facts about the centre and spread hold for every $n$, not just large $n$:

  • The centre stays at $\mu$. Whatever $n$ is, the sample means pile up around $\mu$. This is because $E[\overline{X}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum E[X_i] = \frac{1}{n}\, n\mu = \mu$. Only linearity of expectation is used. Independence is not needed for this line.
  • The spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Quadruple $n$ and the standard deviation of $\overline{X}$ halves. This is because $Var(\overline{X}) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum Var(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2}\, n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$. The step "variance of a sum equals sum of variances" is where independence is used.

When the population is itself normal, no approximation is needed: $\overline{X}$ is exactly normal for every $n$, because sums of independent normals are normal (NCT Chapter 5, Oct 20).

Set $n = 1$, draw 1,000, and compare the lower histogram with the population. Now set $n = 5$ and draw 1,000. Without looking at the table, predict the standard deviation of the sample means from $\sigma$. Then check. Then $n = 20$: by what factor should the spread have shrunk relative to $n = 5$?
At $n = 5$ the normal overlay does not fit well in the right tail. At $n = 30$ it does. Which assumption of the CLT is doing the work there, and why does a skewed population need a larger $n$ than a symmetric one?
The simulation's variance of $\overline{X}$ sits very close to $\sigma^2/n$. If the samples were drawn without replacement from a small population, which line of the variance derivation would fail, and in which direction would the simulated variance move?

4. Using the CLT

Once $\overline{X} \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \normal{\mu}{\sigma^2/n}$, we can compute probabilities about the sample mean using $\Phi$, as in NCT Chapter 5, with $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ in the denominator instead of $\sigma$: $$Pr(\overline{X} \le c) \;\approx\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{c - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right).$$
