1Motivation

Our objective is to say something about the underlying truth, or population parameters. Here is an example.

EXAMPLE: WAGES

You work for a government agency and want to understand the distribution of hourly wages in Canada.

Let \(X\) be the hourly wage of an employee: \(X \sim f_X\). We don't know the PDF \(f_X\).

This distribution can be summarized in several ways:

  • Central tendency: mean, median
  • Dispersion: variance, standard deviation, interquartile range (IQR)
  • Other aspects: for example, skewness

In this course, we will focus on the population mean, here the mean wage \(\mu\). As a measure of central tendency (or "typical" values), it is informative about the underlying distribution. It also connects with economic concepts such as efficiency. Fixing hours worked (the same for everyone), multiply the mean hourly wage by those hours and by the number of people and you get total income, so the mean tells us about the size of the pie. If the pie gets bigger, we might be able to find transfers from those who gain to those who lose so that everyone is at least as well off and someone is better off (a Pareto improvement). We'll discuss on Nov 5 (NCT Chapter 8) how we can model differences between populations using the mean difference.

2We can't see the whole population, so we sample

To learn about \(\mu\) we need data on wages. Getting information on everyone in the population is expensive, or may even be infeasible. So we take a random sample from the population. The idea is to cover the units in the population well enough to build a representative picture of what's going on.

Statistics Canada does this for wages. The Labour Force Survey asks a random sample of households about work each month, rather than asking everyone.

3Probability, inference, and the map

We want to go from the data back to the truth. To do that, we first need to know how the data behave when the truth is known. That is probability. Going the other way is inference.
